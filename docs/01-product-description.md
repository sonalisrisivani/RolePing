# Role Ping — Product description and strategy

## Origin and direction

The founder has access to an engineering college through their father, who is its dean. The original idea was a resume maker, but the concern was that students would register and rarely return. The discussion explored recurring student needs and then converged on personalised job discovery and delivery.

The intended product reduces repeated searching across job boards and company career pages. Students specify skills, role interests, salary expectations, preferred and excluded locations, and other eligibility details. The service collects opportunities centrally, evaluates them, and sends useful matches through a channel the student already uses.

The user proposed a free scheduled digest, for example five jobs at 7 a.m., and paid faster alerts. Those commercial concepts remain hypotheses to validate. The recommendation is to send **up to** five eligible jobs, never fill a quota with unsuitable listings.

## Problem

Students repeatedly spend time discovering openings, opening duplicates, checking whether a role accepts freshers, reviewing location and compensation, and deciding whether a posting is still usable. Search titles can be misleading: an internship may demand professional experience; a location may be incomplete; an old role may be reposted. Existing alerts may not incorporate branch, graduation year, availability, or explicit exclusions.

The product should answer: “Which opportunities are worth my attention today, and why?” It cannot guarantee employment, employer response, or the advantage of being an early applicant.

## Audience

Proposed initial segment: engineering students and recent graduates seeking internships or entry-level employment in India. Start with one college cohort and a limited role/location set. Software roles are a possible initial focus, not a requirement already settled by the user. The benchmark also included graduate engineering trainee roles across disciplines.

Personas:

- Final-year student seeking a first job, with project experience and a fixed graduation year.
- Pre-final-year student seeking an internship with specific availability and location constraints.
- Recent graduate seeking fresher roles, with a minimum compensation preference and excluded locations.
- Operator maintaining employer sources, investigating broken connectors, and resolving incorrect records.

College administrators are distribution/pilot partners initially, not automatically viewers of private student activity. Experienced professionals, recruiters, and institutional dashboards are later possibilities.

## Value proposition

“Tell us what you want. Receive fresh, relevant opportunities with clear eligibility checks and application links, without repeating the search every day.”

Value comes from reduced search effort, fewer unsuitable recommendations, accurate uncertainty labels, and convenient delivery. A notification is useful only if its contents are useful. Site visits, streaks, message volume, mandatory registrations, and review counts are not substitutes for that value.

## Differentiation to prove

1. Eligibility that understands student constraints, not just skill keywords.
2. Explicit explanations and caveats: matches location; graduation year unconfirmed; salary undisclosed.
3. Duplicate suppression and sensible status checks before delivery.
4. Useful college-specific employer coverage, augmented with off-campus sources.
5. User-controlled delivery frequency and an easy way to improve recommendations.

These are proposed differentiators. Competitors may offer overlapping features; the pilot must demonstrate the quality of this implementation.

## Distribution and retention

Use the college to recruit a voluntary pilot, interview students, and identify historical recruiting employers. The user considered mandatory registration. The recommended validation method is voluntary participation; if registration becomes mandatory, report voluntary meaningful activity separately. Any essential college workflow should not depend on a student's paid upgrade.

Word of mouth should follow useful outcomes: a student shares a public job card or invites a friend to create their own profile. Shared cards must not expose the sender's salary preferences, resume, match explanation, or private application history. Ask for honest feedback after a useful interaction; do not tie access to positive reviews.

Job-search retention is naturally episodic. A user who gets hired may pause successfully. Track this separately from users who leave because alerts are poor. New batches, referrals, and later reactivation may be more meaningful than indefinite daily engagement.

## Monetisation hypothesis

Free: daily small digest, one initial channel, basic matching, save/apply actions, and feedback. Paid: faster alerts on newly detected strong matches, multiple searches, company watchlists, and additional delivery controls. Future college subscription: operations/reporting value with suitable privacy boundaries.

Prices, trial length, billing provider, refunds, and institution contracts remain undecided. Payment does not imply a job guarantee, a lower eligibility threshold, or exclusive ownership of public vacancies. Paid delivery should add convenience without intentionally misrepresenting freshness to free users.

Unit economics must include collection, hosting, database, AI if used, messaging, payment fees, and support. Compare costs per active seeker and per useful recommendation, not merely per registered account.
